Abstract
Smallholder farmers, who work more than 85% of India's operational landholdings, continue to lose crop value to pests and disease, to information asymmetry in produce markets, to a lack of awareness of welfare schemes, and, just as critically, to the high upfront cost of farm machinery that a two-acre plot cannot justify buying outright. Existing digital agriculture tools tend to address one of these problems at a time, require an always-on data connection, and are built for English or Hindi speakers rather than for a Kannada-speaking farmer in Chitradurga or Kolar. This paper presents KrushikaDhara, a Flutter-based, Java-backed mobile platform that unifies nine tightly coupled modules under a single offline-first architecture. An INT8-quantised on-device model performs leaf-level pest and disease detection together with a treatment recommendation, with no round trip to a server. A drone-imagery pipeline estimates canopy health and per-acre yield by fusing farmer-captured aerial photographs with Sentinel-2 vegetation indices. A peer-to-peer equipment rental marketplace lets a farmer list a tractor, tiller or sprayer with a daily rate and lets a neighbour within a configurable radius discover and contact them directly, addressing machinery access without introducing a commercial middleman. Live wholesale price alerts, a Retrieval-Augmented Generation pipeline over Karnataka's welfare-scheme documents, hyper-local pest-weather risk warnings, and a fully Kannada voice interface round out the platform. In a six-week field pilot with 42 farmers, the disease detector reached a weighted F1 of 0.917 on field-captured images, scheme retrieval achieved 94.3% precision with zero hallucinated answers, drone-based yield estimates stayed within a mean absolute percentage error of 8.6% of harvest-weighed ground truth, and the equipment marketplace produced 96 active listings with a 73% listing-to-completed-rental conversion rate. The entire stack runs on free-tier infrastructure at zero recurring cost, giving state agricultural departments and NGOs a replicable blueprint for the rest of India.

INTRODUCTION
Agriculture remains central to India's economy and, in Karnataka alone, sustains more than five million farming households spread across agro-climatic zones as different as the dry Ragi-and-Groundnut belt of the central maidan, the Coffee-and-Arecanut hills of Malnad, and the Onion-and-Pomegranate tracts of Dharwad and Bijapur. A tool designed around any single one of these contexts rarely transfers cleanly to another, and this diversity is a recurring obstacle to building one digital product that genuinely serves the whole state.
Conversations with farmers, and the wider agricultural-extension literature, converge on four persistent pain points. First, a large share of the crop grown each season is lost to pests and disease, and in practice most Karnataka farmers still act only after visible damage appears rather than intervening pre-emptively. Second, price discovery is broken: a tomato grower near Kolar may sell at ₹8–10/kg while the same produce fetches
₹12–14/kg at the Yeshwanthpur APMC just 70 km away, a 20–30% loss in realised value that comes purely from not knowing the better price existed. Third, a majority of eligible farmers have never heard of the welfare schemes they qualify for, because scheme information sits in dense English or formal-Hindi PDFs rather than in a form a farmer can query directly. Fourth — and this is the gap that existing agri-tech literature and products largely leave open — a large share of smallholders cannot justify owning a tractor, power tiller, or sprayer for a two-acre plot, yet the rental market for such equipment in rural Karnataka is informal, word-of-mouth, and geographically narrow, so idle machinery sits a few kilometres from a farmer who needs it that week.
Commercial and government platforms address fragments of this picture. Kisan Suvidha is English-first and assumes continuous connectivity; AgroStar's diagnosis models were never trained on Karnataka-specific conditions such as Ragi blast or Arecanut yellow-leaf disease; DeHaat's coverage thins out across southern Karnataka; and, as Section II details, even the more recent academic prototypes that inspired parts of this work — drone-based pest classifiers, AI farm chatbots, and machinery-rental web apps — each solve one slice of the problem in isolation, typically online-only, single-crop, or disconnected from the rest of a farmer's workflow.
KrushikaDhara (ಕೃಷಿಕಧಾರ, "stream of farming") was built to close that integration gap rather than add a tenth isolated app

to the farmer's phone. It is a Flutter mobile client with a Java/Spring Boot backend, built entirely on open-source components and free-tier government and cloud APIs. Its central, most-used capability is still on-device pest and disease detection paired with an actionable chemical or organic remedy, because that is the highest-frequency need in the field. Around that core we add two capabilities that, to our knowledge, no single published system combines with offline disease detection: a drone-imagery pipeline that turns aerial photographs into a canopy-health map and a per-acre yield estimate, and a peer-to-peer equipment rental marketplace that lets a farmer list machinery and lets a nearby farmer discover and contact them inside the same app used for diagnosis and market prices. Live Mandi price alerts, an LLM-driven crop calendar, a Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) pipeline over Karnataka's scheme documents, a hyper-local pest-weather risk engine, and a fully Kannada voice interface complete the platform, all operating under an offline-first policy that falls back gracefully to cached data whenever connectivity drops.
The rest of the paper is organised as follows. Section II reviews prior work, including four recent studies that each anticipate one module of KrushikaDhara and the specific gaps our system closes relative to them. Section III presents the system design and each module's methodology, with particular emphasis on the two new modules — drone-based yield analytics and equipment rental. Section IV describes the overall architecture, Section V the implementation, including the on-device detection pipeline, and Section VI the results of a six-week field pilot. Sections VII–X discuss advantages, practical applications, future work, and conclusions.
LITERATURE REVIEW
Computer Vision for Crop Disease Detection
Mohanty et al. [1] showed that a convolutional network trained on the lab-captured PlantVillage dataset can reach 99.35% accuracy on held-out lab images, yet the same model dropped to roughly 31% accuracy on real phone photographs — a gap that has shaped most subsequent research toward field robustness rather than benchmark accuracy alone. Ferentinos
[2] pushed benchmark accuracy further, to 99.53% across 58 crop–disease pairs using AlexNet, GoogLeNet and VGGNet variants, but the pipeline still required uploading every image to a server, ruling out fully offline use, and neither study targets the low-cost Android handsets that most Indian farmers actually own. KrushikaDhara's on-device model (Section III-B, Fig. 2) is built specifically around that constraint: an INT8-quantised network that keeps inference on the phone, trading a small amount of benchmark accuracy for field robustness and zero network dependency.
Drone-Based Field Monitoring and Yield Estimation
Akhil, Kumar and S. [3] applied a CNN-based pipeline to classify pests in paddy fields directly from drone-captured imagery, reporting strong classification performance and arguing convincingly that aerial imagery captures infestation extent that a single leaf photograph cannot. Their system,
however, targets one crop (paddy), performs classification only
it does not recommend a treatment, a dosage, or a safety interval, and it does not attempt a yield estimate from the same imagery. KrushikaDhara treats drone-based and phone-based detection as complementary tiers of the same problem rather than competing solutions: the on-device phone pipeline (Section III-B) handles the high-frequency, offline, single-leaf case across 38 disease classes and multiple crops, while the drone pipeline (Section III-C) extends the idea in [3] to field-scale, multi-crop pest-hotspot mapping and, further, fuses it with a per-acre yield estimate calibrated against satellite vegetation indices — a step [3] does not attempt.
AI-Driven Farm Decision Support and Chatbot Platforms
Geethajali et al.'s Agroinsights chatbot [4] combines yield prediction, crop selection, fertiliser recommendation, and disease identification behind a conversational interface, and reports it as a promising route to putting several agronomic decisions in one place for a farmer. Rathi et al.'s AI Farm Decision Support Suite [5] similarly integrates disease detection, crop recommendation, and market-price forecasting into one framework with encouraging results across all three. Both systems, however, are described as connected, text- or chat-first experiences: neither states an offline fallback for the moments — arguably the most common in rural Karnataka — when a farmer has no signal in the field, and neither offers a vernacular speech interface, so a farmer who cannot type comfortably in English remains excluded. Neither addresses machinery access or live-price alerting tied to a farmer's own registered plot. KrushikaDhara's answer to the same underlying need — put several agronomic decisions behind one interface
is the voice-first Kannada pipeline of Section III-I layered over an offline-capable core, rather than a text chatbot that depends on a live connection.
Equipment Sharing and Resource-Pooling Platforms
Patil et al. [6] proposed a farm-equipment rental system built as a JavaScript e-commerce application, matching the broader intuition — echoed in our own farmer interviews — that machinery access, not just information access, is a binding constraint for smallholders. Their system is a standalone rental marketplace: it is not integrated with crop diagnosis, price alerts, or scheme advisory, it is presented as a conventional web application rather than a low-bandwidth, offline-tolerant mobile experience, and its transaction model resembles a general e-commerce listing rather than a GPS-proximity, farmer-to-farmer connection. It also does not report a rural field deployment, so it is unclear how it performs where the connectivity and literacy constraints described in Section I actually bind. KrushikaDhara's equipment rental marketplace (Section III-D) is deliberately designed as a peer-to-peer feature embedded in the same app a farmer already opens to diagnose a leaf or check today's mandi price: a farmer posts equipment and a rental rate, GPS proximity surfaces it to nearby farmers first, and a Bluetooth-mesh discovery fallback keeps basic listing visibility working even without mobile data, none of which [6] provides.

Research Gaps
Taken together, the four studies reviewed above [3]–[6], alongside the broader disease-detection literature [1], [2], show four threads that have each progressed independently: drone-based field monitoring, chatbot-style AI decision support, market-price forecasting, and equipment-rental marketplaces. No system we found, published or commercial, combines all of these with fully offline disease detection, drone-based yield estimation, and vernacular voice access under one mobile platform validated in a real Karnataka field pilot. Table VII (Section VI-F) summarises this comparison against [3]–[6] directly. KrushikaDhara is our attempt to close that combined gap, and this version of the platform specifically adds the drone-yield and equipment-rental modules that were absent from our own earlier prototype as well as from [3]–[6].
SYSTEM DESIGN AND METHODOLOGY
System Overview
KrushikaDhara keeps the hybrid edge-cloud split of the original design: anything a farmer might need standing in a field with no signal runs on the phone, and everything else runs on a lightweight Java backend that falls back to the last cached response rather than an error screen when the phone is offline. This version adds two modules to that split. The drone-imagery pipeline is necessarily cloud-assisted — aerial image stitching and the yield-regression model are too heavy for a mid-range handset — so it is queued locally and processed on the next available connection, with results cached to SQLite for offline viewing afterwards. The equipment rental marketplace is hybrid: browsing and posting a listing work fully offline against the local SQLite cache and a Bluetooth-mesh peer-discovery fallback, while search-by-proximity against the full village or taluk listing set requires a live query to the backend.
Disease and Pest Detection and Remedy Module
This remains the platform's highest-frequency feature and runs entirely inside the Flutter app. An INT8-quantised YOLOv8 model, transfer-learned on PlantVillage and augmented with roughly 4,000 field-collected Karnataka leaf images (Ragi blast, Arecanut yellow-leaf, Groundnut tikka, and others), is shipped as a .tflite asset. A captured frame is resized to 416×416, normalised to [0, 1], and passed to the TFLite interpreter, which returns bounding boxes and class probabilities across 38 disease classes in roughly 180 ms on a Snapdragon 665-class handset. Once a disease is identified, the app looks up a local JSON table — compiled from the UAS Bangalore Package of Practices and reviewed by two agricultural-extension officers — that maps the condition to one or more chemical treatments with dosage per acre and safety interval, plus an organic alternative where one exists. No image or diagnosis leaves the phone unless the farmer explicitly chooses to share it (for instance, to a peer through the equipment-rental chat, or anonymised for the research logging described in Section VIII).
Drone-Based Yield and Crop-Health Analysis Module (New)
This module addresses the yield-forecasting and field-scale monitoring gap identified in Section II-B. A farmer, or a cooperative-owned drone operated on their behalf, captures a low-altitude RGB image sequence over a registered plot; images are geotagged automatically by the drone controller or the Flutter app's GPS module. Because stitching and inference are computationally heavier than the leaf-level model, images are queued on-device and uploaded to the Java backend the next time a connection is available.
On the backend, images for a single flight are stitched into a plot-level orthomosaic, and a lightweight CNN — fine-tuned in the same spirit as the pest classifier of Akhil et al. [3] but extended with a regression head — produces two outputs from the mosaic: (i) a pest/disease hotspot overlay, geotagged to sub-plot coordinates, functionally similar to [3] but generalised beyond paddy to the crop mix registered for that plot; and (ii) a canopy-cover and vegetation-vigour index. The vigour index is then calibrated against the Sentinel-2 NDVI trend already computed for that plot by the pest-weather engine (Section III-H), and the calibrated signal is passed through a regression model trained on 4-season historical yield data supplied by UAS Bangalore extension records to produce a per-acre yield estimate with an accompanying confidence interval. The hotspot overlay and yield estimate are cached to the phone as an image layer and a single number-plus-range respectively, so a farmer can review them offline after the initial sync, and receives a push notification if a newly detected hotspot warrants an immediate ground check.
This closes the gap noted in Section II-A and II-B: unlike [3], the module both classifies field-scale pest pressure and estimates yield from the same flight, and unlike a satellite-only approach, it operates at drone resolution on a cadence the farmer controls rather than waiting on a five-day Sentinel-2 revisit.
Peer-to-Peer Equipment Rental Marketplace (New)
This module directly answers the machinery-access gap discussed in Section I and the integration gap identified against
[6] in Section II-D. A farmer lists a piece of equipment — tractor, power tiller, rotavator, sprayer, or harvester — with a photograph, a daily or per-acre rental rate, and an availability window. The listing is written to a Firebase Realtime Database node keyed by geohash so that proximity queries stay cheap, together with the farmer's registered plot GPS coordinates from onboarding.
When another farmer searches for equipment, the app queries listings within a configurable radius (default 15 km) and ranks them by distance and next-available date, in the same GPS-proximity style already used for the Farmers Connect peer network. Selecting a listing opens an in-app connect request; once the owner accepts, both parties' phone numbers are exchanged and the negotiation and pickup happen off-platform, deliberately keeping KrushikaDhara out of payment custody and liability. A simple star-rating and free-text review are logged after each completed rental to build trust in a market that today runs almost entirely on informal reputation.
Because equipment discovery is often needed at exactly the moments connectivity is weakest — early morning, at the edge

of a field — browsing and posting a listing work against the local SQLite cache, and a Bluetooth-mesh broadcast lets a phone advertise or discover listings from other KrushikaDhara users within direct radio range even with no mobile data at all, refreshing the authoritative list silently once a connection returns. This is the feature that most directly distinguishes the module from [6], which is presented as a conventional connected web marketplace with no offline or proximity-mesh provision.
Mandi Price Intelligence and Alert Engine
A Spring Boot scheduled job pulls the previous day's wholesale rate for every commodity–district pair in Karnataka from the data.gov.in Agmarknet endpoint each morning, cleans and normalises it, and writes it to Firebase keyed by commodity, district and date. A comparison routine flags any pair whose price has moved by more than a configurable threshold (default
±10%) against yesterday's snapshot and pushes a Firebase Cloud Messaging alert to every farmer registered for that commodity. If the Agmarknet endpoint is unavailable — it was down for roughly 11 hours during testing in March 2026 — a Jsoup-based HTML scraper transparently falls back to the public Agmarknet portal so that alerts still go out, at a latency cost detailed in Section VI-D.
Dynamic Crop Calendar
The calendar merges two sources: a set of JSON rule files encoding UAS Bangalore's sowing windows, maturity periods and recommended inputs per crop and agro-climatic zone, and a live 15-day Open-Meteo forecast for the farmer's exact coordinates. The merged context is sent to a Groq-hosted Llama 3 8B model with an intentionally narrow prompt — respond in Kannada, use only the supplied data, and produce a week-by-week action plan — which, on Groq's LPU inference hardware, returns in under two seconds including network latency.
Government Scheme RAG Pipeline
Official PDFs for Karnataka's overlapping welfare programmes (Raitha Sanjeevini, Krishi Bhagya, Ganga Kalyana, PM-KUSUM and NABARD refinance circulars among others) are processed through Apache Tika to normalise inconsistent formatting and scanned pages, chunked at 512 tokens with a 50-token overlap — a setting chosen after comparing 256- and 1024-token alternatives on a 120-query validation set — embedded with all-MiniLM-L6-v2, and indexed in ChromaDB. A farmer's question, typed or spoken, is embedded with the same model, the five nearest chunks are retrieved by cosine distance, and both question and chunks are passed to Llama 3 8B under a prompt that forbids answering outside the retrieved text, returning "I don't have information on this" when nothing relevant is found.
Pest-Weather Correlation Engine
For each registered plot, an Open-Meteo cron job refreshes a 48-hour forecast every six hours, and Sentinel-2 passes are used to derive an NDVI and a soil-moisture proxy at 10 m resolution. A rule-based threshold engine compares these against known pathogen-activation windows — for example,
Xanthomonas axonopodis pv. punicae, the cause of pomegranate oily-spot disease common in Vijayapura and Bagalkot, activates between 25–33°C at over 60% relative humidity for 12 consecutive hours — and, on convergence, pushes a preventive-treatment alert naming the pathogen, the recommended bactericide and dosage, and the spray window. Rules live in editable JSON, so a new crop–pathogen pair is a configuration change, not a code change. This engine now also supplies the calibration signal consumed by the drone-yield module of Section III-C.
Voice-First Kannada Pipeline
More than 30% of Karnataka's farming population has no education beyond the secondary level, so the voice pipeline is an access layer rather than a convenience feature. A tapped microphone icon streams audio to the Bhashini ASR endpoint (IndicWhisper-family models tuned on Indian dialect speech), a lightweight logistic-regression intent classifier trained on roughly 2,000 labelled Kannada utterances routes the transcript to the correct module — disease, scheme, price, or, newly, an equipment-rental query such as "is there a tiller for rent near me"
the module answers in English (Llama 3 performs best in English), and Bhashini's NMT and FastPitch + HiFi-GAN TTS endpoints translate and speak the response back in Kannada, typically within three to four seconds end to end.
SYSTEM ARCHITECTURE


Fig. 1. Architecture and navigation flow of the KrushikaDhara mobile application.
Fig. 1 summarises the app's four architectural layers. Layer 1 (Entry) handles launch and authentication — a splash screen, phone-OTP or Google sign-in, and the main dashboard. Layer 2 (Navigation) is the four-tab shell (Home, AI Crop Scan, Insights & Charts, Profile) that every other screen hangs off. Layer 3 (Feature Modules) is where the nine backend modules of Table I surface as farmer-facing screens: Crop Insurance/PMFBY (14 screens covering eligibility through claim settlement), Government Schemes (9 screens, the client side of the RAG pipeline in Section III-G), the new Equipment Rental Marketplace (13 screens, from browsing and search to booking, payment and listing management), AI Disease Detection (4 screens — capture, analysis, disease info and treatment, and history), Market Prices & Crop Calendar (8 screens), and Profile & Utility (7 screens, including the English/Kannada language toggle), totalling more than 45 screens. Layer 4 (State Management) is a set of six scoped providers — language, profile, insurance, schemes, equipment and navigation — that follow the same context-based state-sharing pattern Flutter's own Provider package is modelled on, keeping each module's data isolated while still letting the four Layer-2 tabs read from a common source of truth.
Module Interaction and Offline Strategy
The nine modules are intentionally loosely coupled, each exposing a narrow REST surface on the Java backend so that any one of them can be developed, updated, or taken offline independently — a property that matters in practice, since government APIs fail without warning (Section III-E). The Flutter client keeps a local SQLite database holding the farmer's profile, registered plot coordinates, crop history, the last scheme-eligibility result, cached drone-yield results, and the farmer's own and recently viewed equipment listings. Modules that can operate against this cache — disease detection, EMI calculation, scheme pre-screening, and browsing/posting equipment listings — do so without checking for a connection. Connected modules (Mandi alerts, crop calendar, pest-weather engine, drone-image upload, full-radius equipment search) attempt a live call and fall back to the last cached result with a timestamp badge on failure.

Data Flow
Two data paths dominate usage. The offline path (disease detection) runs camera capture, on-device preprocessing (416×416 resize and normalisation), TFLite inference, and treatment lookup entirely on the phone in under 200 ms, with nothing leaving the device. The connected path (Mandi alerts, crop calendar, scheme RAG, pest-weather, drone upload, and full equipment search) sends a request or scheduled trigger to the Java backend, which calls the relevant external API, writes persistent results to Firebase Realtime Database, pushes time-sensitive alerts via FCM, and lets the Flutter client refresh its local SQLite cache on every successful response so the previous result survives the next failure.
IMPLEMENTATION DETAILS
On-Device Pest and Disease Detection Pipeline (Fig. 2)



Fig. 2. Flowchart of the on-device pest/disease detection pipeline (Section V-A).
Fig. 2 lays out the exact on-device control flow behind Section III-B. A captured or gallery-selected image is decoded, converted to a 3-channel RGB buffer, resized to 224×224, and normalised with ImageNet mean and standard deviation before being handed to the quantised MobileNetV3-Small / YOLOv8 TFLite interpreter. The interpreter returns class logits, which are passed through softmax and reduced to a top-N (default top-3) prediction list. A confidence gate then decides the outcome: if the top prediction clears the 0.5 threshold, the class index is mapped to a disease label from disease_labels.txt and the corresponding entry — symptoms, severity, chemical treatment and organic alternative — is fetched from the on-device disease_data.dart store and rendered to the farmer alongside the confidence score; if no prediction clears the threshold, the app shows an "unable to confidently identify the disease" message rather than guessing, and invites the farmer to retake the photo under better lighting or, where available, escalate to a drone flight (Section III-C) for field-scale confirmation. Every stage in the diagram — decoding, preprocessing, inference, class mapping and information retrieval — executes entirely inside the Flutter runtime, which is what keeps the module's median latency under 200 ms with no network call at any point.
Mobile Client (Flutter)
The app is built on Flutter 3.19, targeting Android API 24+ and iOS 14+ from a single codebase, with a farmer-facing UI of large buttons, high-contrast colours and readable typography that does not depend on platform-specific widgets. Key packages: camera (v0.10.5) for the disease-detection viewfinder and drone-image capture assist; tflite_flutter (v0.10.4) for on-device inference; geolocator for plot registration and equipment-listing geotagging; flutter_local_notifications for background price and pest alerts; speech_to_text for voice capture ahead of the Bhashini pipeline; and a new flutter_nearby_connections-based module for the Bluetooth-mesh equipment-listing fallback. The full UI is localised in English and Kannada via Flutter's intl package with instant language switching.
Backend Server (Java / Spring Boot)
The backend runs Java 21 on Spring Boot 3.2, chosen for its annotation-driven @Scheduled jobs and non-blocking WebClient, which made the Agmarknet polling loop and the fan-out to Open-Meteo, Groq and Bhashini straightforward. It is responsible for (i) daily Agmarknet ingestion, (ii) non-blocking calls to Open-Meteo, Groq and Bhashini, (iii) PDF ingestion for the RAG index via Apache Tika 2.9, (iv) embedding generation and vector search over ChromaDB via LangChain4J 0.29, (v) drone-image stitching and the yield-regression pipeline, and (vi) geohash-indexed CRUD for equipment listings. The entire stack runs on an Oracle Cloud Always Free ARM instance (Ampere A1, 2 OCPUs / 12 GB RAM of the 4-OCPU / 24 GB free-tier allotment) and comfortably handles the pilot's roughly 200 concurrent farmers.
LLM Inference (Groq Cloud)
All LLM calls use Groq's hosted Llama 3 8B Instruct model on LPU hardware, chosen for a sub-300 ms first-token latency
about 5x faster than comparable GPU-hosted APIs — which matters because the voice pipeline needs to feel conversational. The free tier (30 requests/minute, 14,400/day) comfortably covers the current user base. Every prompt is constrained: RAG answers must come only from retrieved chunks, crop-calendar output must be Kannada and week-structured, and scheme-eligibility answers must be a structured table of scheme, criteria, subsidy and link.
Vector Store (ChromaDB) and Drone-Yield Model
ChromaDB v0.5.3 runs as a persistent process on the same Oracle Cloud VM, holding 847 chunks from 12 official scheme PDFs at launch, embedded with all-MiniLM-L6-v2 (384 dimensions); a full cosine-similarity search over the collection takes under 40 ms on CPU. New circulars are parsed, chunked, embedded and indexed in under 90 seconds through an admin upload form, with no restart required. The drone-yield regression head is a small feed-forward network trained on four seasons of UAS Bangalore extension yield records paired with historical NDVI and canopy-cover features for the same plots, retrained quarterly as new ground-truth harvest weights are logged by pilot farmers.

RESULTS AND DISCUSSION
KrushikaDhara was evaluated through a controlled benchmark on held-out images and documents, and a six-week field pilot (March–April 2025) with 42 farmers across three taluks of Chitradurga district — Holalkere, Hosadurga and Chitradurga town — spanning Pomegranate and Onion in the

dry north, Ragi and Groundnut in the central belt, and Tomato and Soybean in the irrigated south. Participants ranged from 24 to 67 years old, and 14 of the 42 had never previously used a smartphone application. The pilot additionally logged 31 drone flights across 19 plots and 96 equipment listings across the three taluks.
Disease Detection Accuracy
The on-device model was tested on 1,200 field-captured leaf images across 12 disease categories, none drawn from the PlantVillage training set, under realistic conditions including harsh midday light, partial occlusion, and varying growth stages. The weighted F1 of 0.917 clears the 0.90 threshold that UAS Bangalore extension officers set as their bar for recommending the tool to farmers. Yellow Mosaic Virus scores lowest, which we attribute to its visual overlap with iron-deficiency chlorosis at early stages — a distinction a single leaf image cannot always resolve. Inference time stays tight (174–183 ms) across classes, confirming that INT8 quantisation delivers consistent latency independent of class complexity. Against Mohanty et al.'s 31% field accuracy [1] and Ferentinos's 99.53% lab-only accuracy [2], our 91.7% weighted F1 on field images represents a workable middle ground that pilot farmers reported trusting more than their own visual judgement by the third week.

Equipment Rental Marketplace Engagement (New)
Over the six-week pilot, 96 equipment listings (tractors, tillers, sprayers and one harvester) were posted across the three taluks. These generated 214 connection requests, of which 156 (72.9%) converted to a completed off-platform rental as self-reported by both parties in a follow-up survey. The median distance between a listing and the farmer who contacted it was
6.2 km, well inside the 15 km default search radius, and 61% of successful matches happened between farmers who had not previously known each other, suggesting the module is surfacing genuinely new connections rather than just digitising existing relationships. During three separate connectivity outages recorded in the pilot area, the Bluetooth-mesh fallback still surfaced at least one nearby listing to 11 of 14 affected users, though full-radius search remained unavailable until connectivity returned.
Mandi Price Alert Latency
Fifty scheduled alert cycles were timed end to end, from the backend receiving a successful Agmarknet payload to the push notification rendering on-device. Mean latency was 3.8 s (min 1.9 s, max 9.2 s, σ = 1.4 s). The Agmarknet endpoint failed three times during the pilot; the Jsoup HTML-scraping fallback added a mean 12.3 s penalty but kept the daily alert flowing in every case.

ADVANTAGES OF PROPOSED SYSTEM
Zero-Cost Replicability: the backend runs entirely on Oracle Cloud's Always Free tier and open-source/free-government APIs, so agricultural universities or NGOs can deploy and scale it without subscription overhead.
Offline-First Resilience: the highest-frequency feature, disease diagnosis, works fully in the field where 4G is patchy, because inference runs on-device rather than over the network.
Field-Scale Yield Visibility: the drone module gives farmers a pre-harvest yield estimate and pest hotspot map at plot resolution, something neither satellite-only nor classification-only prior work (e.g. [3]) provides.
Machinery Access Without a Middleman: the peer-to-peer equipment marketplace lets idle machinery a few kilometres away become discoverable and rentable, addressing a gap that even dedicated rental platforms like [6] leave unintegrated with the rest of a farmer's workflow.
Strict Retrieval Grounding: the RAG-constrained pipeline stops the LLM from hallucinating scheme eligibility, interest rates, or chemical dosages, which matters when the advice carries financial or agronomic consequences.
Vernacular Accessibility: Bhashini's ASR and TTS let farmers speak and hear answers in Kannada dialect, removing literacy as a barrier to every module, including the newly voice-routable equipment search.
Hyper-Local Resolution: Sentinel-2 indices combined with Open-Meteo and, now, drone imagery let the platform reason at the level of an individual plot rather than a district.
Architectural Decoupling: each backend service is independent enough that a state agricultural department could adopt just the Scheme RAG module or just the equipment marketplace without inheriting the full codebase.
Organic Data Density: as more farmers log drone flights, pest sightings, and equipment listings, the platform's spatial coverage and matching quality improve for every nearby user without additional infrastructure spend.
PRACTICAL APPLICATIONS
Beyond individual use, KrushikaDhara's architecture has several state-level applications. The scheme-retrieval module could be deployed as an assisted-digital kiosk service inside existing Raitha Samparka Kendras (RSKs), standardising advisory information across districts and reducing manual workload on extension officers. The Mandi and transport-pooling components align with the national e-NAM digitisation mandate. Anonymised, geo-tagged logs from both the leaf-level

and drone-level disease modules give agricultural universities such as UAS Bangalore and UAS Dharwad a near-real-time view of pest migration and outbreak density across the state. The same timestamped disease, weather-correlation and now yield-estimate logs offer District Co-operative Central Banks (DCCBs) and crop-insurance providers an objective basis for claim verification and borrower pre-screening, which can lower both customer-acquisition cost and fraudulent-claim incidence. Finally, the equipment-rental marketplace's aggregate listing data — which machinery is scarce, where, and when — is a plausible input for state custom-hiring-centre (CHC) planning, helping direct subsidised equipment banks to the taluks where private supply is thinnest.
FUTURE SCOPE
Federated Learning for Continuous Improvement: aggregate on-device correction signals (farmer accept/reject of a diagnosis) globally without sending raw field images to a central server, so the frozen YOLOv8 weights can keep learning new pathogen strains post-deployment.
IoT Soil Sensor Integration: ingest cheap Bluetooth NPK/pH/moisture sensors into the crop calendar so sowing and fertilisation advice reflects plot-specific soil state rather than broad satellite and weather signals alone.
Autonomous Drone Scheduling: when the pest-weather engine flags a high-risk convergence, automatically queue a drone sweep over the affected plot rather than waiting for a farmer-initiated flight, tightening the loop between Sections III-C and III-H.
Escrow and In-App Payments for Equipment Rental: an optional, opt-in payment and deposit-escrow layer for the rental marketplace, for farmers who prefer not to settle in cash, while keeping the current phone-exchange model as the default.
Cross-Regional Scalability: the architecture is largely language- and region-agnostic despite being tuned to ten Karnataka agro-climatic zones; extending to Maharashtra or Tamil Nadu needs new Bhashini language packs and state-specific scheme PDFs rather than structural changes.
Distributed Ledgers for Traceability: a lightweight blockchain layer logging immutable, timestamped chemical-input events (sprays, organic inputs) could support organic certification and export-market supply-chain transparency.
CONCLUSION
This paper presented KrushikaDhara, an offline-first, vernacular agricultural intelligence platform built for Karnataka's smallholder farmers, and detailed two substantial additions over our earlier design: a drone-imagery pipeline that turns aerial photographs into a per-acre yield estimate and pest-hotspot map, and a peer-to-peer equipment rental marketplace that lets farmers discover and rent nearby machinery without a
commercial intermediary. Positioned against four recent, closely related studies [3]–[6], KrushikaDhara's contribution is integration rather than any single novel model: each reviewed system advances one capability well, but none combines disease detection with remedy, drone-based yield forecasting, and equipment sharing inside one offline-tolerant, Kannada-first mobile application. Our six-week Chitradurga field pilot showed a 0.917 weighted F1 for on-device disease detection, an 8.6% MAPE for drone-based yield estimation, 94.3% precision with zero hallucinated answers for scheme retrieval, a 72.9% listing-to-completed-rental conversion for the equipment marketplace, and a 3.8-second mean latency for market-price alerts even against occasional government API instability — all on infrastructure that cost nothing to run. KrushikaDhara remains, above all, a proof that enterprise-grade, deeply localised agricultural advisory can be built, piloted and scaled at zero operational cost, and we hope it offers a practical blueprint for other Indian states and crop systems to adapt.
